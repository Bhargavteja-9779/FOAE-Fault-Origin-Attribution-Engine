\section*{Reviewer 1}

\rc{R1.1 (realism of the faults)} \emph{The faults are simulated; the detector may only learn the simulator.}
\resp We agree that this is the central question and address it in four ways. (i) All healthy
telemetry, the excitation that drives the faults and the usage histories are real; only the
connector fault is modelled, and the detector never sees model internals. (ii) Sixteen parameter
perturbations (Fig.~9): on full-bus data the AUROC stays within 0.84--0.95 and severe-stage AUROC
$\ge 0.97$. (iii) A \emph{structurally different} fault process (bursty Markov chatter with an
energy-based brown-out criterion; Supplement S8): trained on the nominal model, ECTA reaches 0.856
on full-bus data (severe 0.999). (iv) The analytical observability law (Section~VI-C), which
involves no classifier, predicts the measured detectability (Spearman 0.93).
\chg [Section/page references.]

\rc{R1.2 (stronger deep baselines)} \emph{Compare with a Transformer.}
\resp A Transformer encoder baseline is reported in Supplement S10 for all four corpora; it does
not close the gap to ECTA on unseen vehicles.

\rc{R1.3 (statistical significance)}
\resp Paired vehicle-level bootstrap confidence intervals and p-values for ECTA against every
baseline are given in Supplement S11.

\rc{R1.4 (hyperparameters)}
\resp Hyperparameters were fixed before evaluation and are identical across corpora. Supplement S9
shows that seven gradient-boosting configurations change the AUROC by at most a few thousandths.

\section*{Reviewer 2}

\rc{R2.1 (practical false-alarm rate)}
\resp Supplement S12 reports false alarms per hour of real healthy driving at fixed detection
rates. A full-bus tool detecting 90\% of severely worn connectors raises no false alarm in the
held-out healthy data; low-rate polling tools need multi-trip pooling (Fig.~10).

\rc{R2.2 (window length)}
\resp Supplement S13 reports 10-, 30- and 60-s windows; the 30-s choice is not critical.
