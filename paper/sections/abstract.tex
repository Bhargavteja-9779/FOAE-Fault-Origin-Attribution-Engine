\begin{abstract}
The SAE J1962 diagnostic connector has become the permanent mount and power
source of telematics, insurance and fleet devices, yet it was designed for
occasional workshop use. Vibration-induced fretting of its contacts causes
intermittent data loss and device brown-outs that are routinely blamed on
the device or the vehicle. We show that connector wear can be detected,
attributed and, within limits, predicted from the telemetry the attached
tool already records. The proposed excitation-coherent telemetry analysis
(ECTA) combines self-referenced loss and loss-structure features with a
Poisson test of whether losses co-vary with the vehicle's own engine and
road excitation, as a fretting contact's interruptions must. A
physics-informed model maps contact wear to the observable behaviour of
full-bus loggers, sampling loggers and OBD-II polling testers, with four
realistic confounders. All healthy telemetry, excitation and usage are
real: five public datasets, nine vehicles analysed in detail and a
381-vehicle fleet. With leave-one-vehicle-out validation on six vehicles
from three manufacturers, a full-bus tool detects wear with AUROC 0.880
(moderate 0.891, severe 0.995), uniformly across held-out vehicles
(0.869--0.921), and confirms moderate wear within two minutes of driving
(0.94); deep sequence baselines reach 0.646. A first-principles
observability model predicts the detectability of every tool and wear stage
without any classifier (Spearman 0.93). For low-rate polling tools,
excitation coherence separates wear from bus interference (0.947 vs. 0.737)
and lifts fleet-wide detection from 0.704 to 0.775, and a learned health
index warns 88\% of failing connectors with 1.5\% false alarms.
\end{abstract}
